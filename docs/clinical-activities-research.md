# Clinical Bengali: swallowing and memory checks

Prepared 26 October 2026, for a student who is a speech-language pathologist (SLP) and checks swallowing and memory in patients every day. This note says what the new activities are based on, how far to trust it, and what else could be added.

## What the research found [Web]

**Bedside swallow check**
- A clinical swallow evaluation usually has four parts: a chart review, an interview with the patient or family, an exam of the cranial nerves and the mouth, and then trials with food or drink if it is safe.
- The mouth exam looks at the lips, jaw, tongue, cheeks and soft palate. Patients are asked to open the mouth, stick out and move the tongue, make sounds, cough or clear the throat. Gag and cough reflexes may be checked.
- Water tests are common. The Yale Swallow Protocol gives three ounces of water, and a patient passes if they drink it without coughing or choking. Another bedside test gives 50 ml of water in 10 ml amounts and watches oxygen levels.
- Clinicians listen for a wet voice and coughing, and sit the patient as upright as possible.
- **Limits:** bedside screens vary a lot in accuracy (one source gives sensitivity 42% to 92% and specificity 59% to 91%) and miss silent aspiration. I found no single authoritative script, so the role play follows the common structure, not one protocol.

**Memory and orientation checks**
- Standard screens such as the MMSE and MoCA ask the date, month, year, day and place. A common recall task gives a short list of words, asks for them back later, and does not repeat the list. Cues can be given afterward but are scored separately.
- The MoCA uses five words (one source lists face, velvet, church, daisy, red). I could not confirm the MMSE's three words from my search. Commonly it is "apple, table, penny".
- **Bengali versions:** The Bangla adaptations (BAMSE and MMSE-B) were built for Bangladeshi and low-literacy groups. One source says MMSE-B asks for three objects, **banana, bird and taka**, and adds **seasons** to the time questions (day, date, month, year, season). I used different words (blue, sack, mango) because you described blue and sack in your own example. A real clinical screen should use a validated Bengali form, so the app's words are for language practice only.

## How far to trust this

- The role plays and word lists are for **learning the language**, not for clinical use. They are not a validated tool.
- The Bengali wording is my draft. A teacher or a native clinician should check the phrases a patient would actually hear, especially "বিষম খাওয়া" (to choke), "ভেজা আওয়াজ" (wet voice) and the season words.
- The sources are summaries of guides and papers. Check the details with the student's own protocols.

## More activities that could be added [Judgment]

Cheap (use content we already have):
1. **Listen and do.** The app plays a command ("জিভ বের করুন") and the student picks the matching action. It practises listening, which she asked for, and costs nothing to run.
2. **Three-word memory game.** The app says three words, there is a short pause with a counting task, then the student recalls them by choosing or typing. It trains vocabulary and listening together.
3. **Date and day drills.** Answer "আজ কত তারিখ?" and "আজ কী বার?" with Bengali numerals, days, months and the six seasons. This builds on the numbers lesson.
4. **A quick phrase card.** One screen of the 20 commands she uses most, big text and audio, for use at work.

More Bengali to write:
5. **Naming and repeating.** Name objects, repeat a sentence, follow a two-step command ("take the pen and put it on the table").
6. **Word fluency.** "Name as many animals as you can in one minute."
7. **Reading aloud and writing a sentence.** Short sentences for a patient to read or write.
8. **A family meeting.** Explaining a result to the patient's family, building on the home-practice role play.

Not now (costly): scoring a patient's pronunciation or spoken answers with speech recognition or an AI model.
